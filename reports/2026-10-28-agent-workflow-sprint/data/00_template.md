## What the evidence says

1. **The binding constraint is orchestrator quota, not dollars.** The agents' recorded DeepInfra spend over five days was about $1.15 (57 calls, in three places), while Claude was locked out for 62 hours (weekly limit hit 2026-10-26T01:00Z, reset 2026-10-28T15:00Z) after two 5-hour limits. Codex hit 59 unique limit turns in nine days, in bursts of a median 28.5 minutes followed by a median 4.6-hour wait. In the 103-hour window both agents were blocked for up to 59 hours (57%), an upper bound because Codex idle time includes your absences.
2. **The first requests after a limit are mostly cache creation.** After a limit stall Claude's first requests re-create its prompt cache: a median 502.5k tokens, all cache creation (about $4.0 modelled), n=3; Codex's main thread uses 136k. These are tokens before the first productive action under a rule-based classifier: they contain the rebuild and the first productive request, they are not isolated handoff waste, and whether a capsule would save any of it is a hypothesis for experiment E3. The plan a fresh session reads grew from 13k to 31k tokens.
3. **Whether delegation saved orchestrator effort cannot be estimated from this data.** The first pass's "6 to 8 times the delegate's output" came from a population that was mostly commands that merely mentioned a runner, with windows that began before the answers existed; it is withdrawn. Delegation was rebuilt from the runners' own records: 57 development calls, 36 of them launched by a shell call that returned before the API call had finished. The repaired post-result interval is short (Claude median 8.4 minutes and 17.2k tokens per batch, ended by a commit in 18 of 19; Codex 7.6 minutes and 25.2k tokens, n=6) and contains integration and unrelated work, so it is an upper bound and not a verification cost. Changes with a delegate in the commit text cost a median index of 620k against 713k without (14 against 10), confounded by task type.
4. **Delegates did well where acceptance was mechanical and badly where it was not.** Claude landed 9 delegate-drafted commits whose messages record 0 to 2 review fixes (a provenance summary, not measured draft retention), the adapter ones checked by golden fixtures. Codex's two mapper, builder, critic chains got no accepted builder output; the mapper and critic roles were useful.
5. **Review findings held up about half the time in pooled commit records (6 of 12, Wilson 25 to 75%), but the Claude-against-Codex split was a misattribution.** All six commit-recorded reviews were commissioned by Claude; the "four findings" of commit `33fb3b5` came from Claude's own review of a ChatGPT implementation (the reviewer's output was recovered from Claude's scratchpad). Three of five events record only the upheld findings, so 50% is an upper bound, and it is a tally of recorded dispositions, not an estimate of reviewer precision.
6. **The delegation tooling has measurable defects.** One finished call stayed `started` in the ledger; a retry began before the first completed; 4 of 9 completed runner calls hit the output cap; 3 of 8 rate cards are stale (GLM-5.3 is twice the card); development records live in three places and about $0.45 of reader spend is in no ledger. The per-call detail the first pass called missing exists in the Claude scratchpad.
7. **Gemini's review is partly right and partly duplicates existing parts.** Its 943k compaction is real; its re-processing cost argument is overstated (cache reads cost about 4% of fresh input). Most proposed components exist in some form, and the harness specification already defines a context capsule and exact-quote closures. Of its 32 claims about outside companies and tools, 8 are verified, 16 plausible and 8 unverified; 35 more concern our own sessions. I re-read the OpenAI announcement myself (the fetch tool gets HTTP 403, the built-in browser does not).
8. **Hosted compute prior art (`G:\vast`):** 31 jobs for $13.62, but two of six boxes produced nothing and ran about 14 hours (an estimated third of spend) after an error had been logged.
9. **The literature (new in revision 2).** @@LEVELS@@ The papers supply metrics, schemas and cautions, not a cost model for delegation. The findings that bear most on the design: the verifier, not the generator, dominated one system's overhead (#07); a drift-control method scored lowest on constraint compliance and fidelity in all three domains tested outside its own, including code (#01, Table 9: 40% against ReAct's 95%); a hand-coded rule did as well as a full model-based compiler (#14); one robotics safety paper reports false-execution and false-refusal rates against a risk frontier (#08); multi-verifier robustness bounds assume independence that the authors' own data weaken (#09). ## What I propose (details and plan rows in `06-design-proposals.md`)
DELEG-01 runner hygiene and receipt v2 (atomic idempotency, timeout-derived expiry, state separation, one ledger for all agents); DELEG-02 typed, anchored delegate output with a local verifier that records quote validity and claim support separately; DELEG-03 packet lint and a pre-landing diff-scope guard; DELEG-04 resume capsule, a prototype to be tested against restored history before any saving is asserted; DELEG-05 auto-recorded delegation lines and a narrow closure check; then a small local pilot of DELEG-06 (executable gate) and an observational DELEG-07 (limit-aware scheduling); DELEG-08 lane isolation is a prerequisite for concurrent writers. HARNESS-10 should fold into HARNESS-04/06 where they already cover it. I do not propose new FanOutDispatcher, LeaseWatchdog, DeferredQuestionQueue, EngagementPlanner or telemetry components, and model-role routes stay with the existing qualification records.
